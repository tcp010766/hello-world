# 产业链从中国转移到马来西亚: China → Malaysia Supply-Chain Shift, 2015–2026

**Sectors covered:** new-energy batteries (新能源电池), automotive / EV (汽车), specialty and fine chemicals (精密化工), data centres (数据中心)
**As of:** October 2026. Sources are listed at the end. Where reporting is thin or conflicting, the text says so.

---

## 1. Executive summary

Over the last ten years Malaysia has changed from a place that absorbed **low-margin "China+1" overflow** (solar modules, electronics assembly, furniture) into a **destination for strategic Chinese capacity**: lithium-battery cells and materials, right-hand-drive EV assembly, and the largest cluster of Chinese-operated data-centre capacity outside China.

The shift happened in three waves:

| Wave | Period | Main driver | Typical Chinese mover | What moved |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Cost and BRI overflow | 2013–2017 | Chinese overcapacity, Belt and Road financing, Malaysian state partners | SOEs, steel and solar groups | Steel (Alliance Steel, MCKIP), solar wafers/cells/modules, industrial parks |
| 2. Tariff evasion → tariff hedging | 2018–2022 | US Section 301 tariffs, COVID supply shocks | Private electronics, solar, and auto-parts firms | Final assembly and "last substantial transformation" steps for US-bound goods |
| 3. Strategic localisation | 2023–2026 | US/EU de-risking, IRA and FEOC rules, chip export controls, Malaysia's NIMP 2030 and NETR | Battery majors, Geely/BYD, GDS/ByteDance-linked DC operators, materials suppliers | Cell and materials plants, CKD/EV plants, hyperscale DCs, R&D centres |

Headline numbers:
- Malaysia's approved investment hit records three years in a row: **RM378.5bn in 2024** (later revised to about RM384bn) and **RM426.7bn in 2025**, with foreign investment of **RM207.1bn** (+20.9%) in 2025.
- China was the **#2 foreign source in 1H2025 (RM23.4bn)**, behind Singapore. Note that much Chinese money arrives through Singapore and Hong Kong vehicles, so official "China" figures understate it.
- Penang alone approved **RM4.2bn of Chinese manufacturing investment in 2025** and hosts more than 80 Chinese firms.
- Johor went from almost no data-centre capacity in 2020 to roughly **580 MW live and about 5.8 GW in the pipeline by mid-2025**, and is on track for a **1 GW live market by end-2026**.

The main lesson: **the move has been a relocation of the final value-adding steps, with Chinese upstream inputs, capital, equipment and engineers still in the loop.** That works only while rules of origin, export controls and the host country's infrastructure allow it, and 2024–2026 showed each of those can tighten quickly.

## 2. Why Malaysia? The structural pull factors

1. **Geopolitical "neutral" positioning.** Kuala Lumpur deliberately stays friendly with both Washington and Beijing. In November 2025 the trade minister said Malaysia wants access to both NVIDIA and Huawei Ascend chips. It is a credible place for a Chinese firm to serve Western customers.
2. **An existing electronics and semiconductor ecosystem.** Penang has 50 years of back-end semiconductor work (Intel, AMD, Infineon, ASE), which gives it skilled technicians, suppliers and logistics. Malaysia handles about 13% of global chip assembly, test and packaging.
3. **Ethnic Chinese business networks and language.** Roughly 23% of Malaysians are ethnic Chinese, with Mandarin-speaking managers and established intermediaries. That lowers set-up and coordination costs a lot compared with Vietnam, India or Mexico.
4. **Industrial land and power.** Land at Kulim, Batu Kawan, Johor, Kedah and Kuantan was historically cheap and available. Power and water were cheap until 2025.
5. **Policy pull.**
   - **NIMP 2030** (New Industrial Master Plan, 2023) targets EVs, advanced electronics, chemicals and the digital economy.
   - **NETR** (National Energy Transition Roadmap, 2023) supports the battery and renewable-energy value chain.
   - **National Semiconductor Strategy** (2024) sets a RM500bn investment target.
   - **Incentives:** EV CBU import-duty exemptions (temporary, which pushed brands to localise before they expired), pioneer status, investment tax allowances, and data-centre "Green Lane" approvals.
   - **Government-to-government parks:** MCKIP (Malaysia–China Kuantan Industrial Park) is twinned with Qinzhou, Guangxi, under the "Two Countries, Twin Parks" model.
6. **Market access.** RCEP gives cumulative rules of origin across Asia. Malaysia's right-hand-drive market opens ASEAN RHD markets (Thailand, Indonesia, Brunei) and export routes to the UK, Australia and Japan.

## 3. Sector deep-dives

### 3.1 New-energy batteries (新能源电池)

**How the shift happened**

- **Precursor: solar, 2010–2022.** Chinese PV makers (Jinko, LONGi, JA Solar, Trina-linked units) built large plants in Kedah, Penang and Melaka after the 2012 US and EU solar duties on China. This was the template for the battery industry: put the final cell and module steps in Malaysia and keep polysilicon and wafers in China.
- **Wave 3: batteries, 2023–2026.** Chinese lithium-ion makers clustered in **Kedah (Kulim and Gurun)** and **Johor**:
  - **EVE Energy (亿纬锂能):** a Kulim cylindrical-cell plant (21700 format, about 680m cells a year, for power tools and e-two-wheelers) started production in early 2025. It was EVE's first factory outside China. In June 2025 EVE announced up to **RMB 8.65bn (~US$1.2bn)** for an **energy-storage (ESS) battery plant** in Kulim, citing "escalating international trade friction".
  - **Zhuhai CosMX (珠海冠宇):** a RM1bn plant in Kulim for consumer and tool batteries.
  - **Highstar / Pret:** 2.5 GWh a year of cylindrical cells in Johor (announced February 2025).
  - **Materials suppliers followed the cell makers.** Putailai (璞泰来) announced a **US$297m, 50,000 t/yr anode base** at Gurun, Kedah, in March 2026. Hunan Yuneng (cathode, about RM560m) and electrolyte, separator and structural-parts suppliers such as Capchem, Senior, Tenpower and Enjie are also coming.
- **What is not yet there:** large-scale **EV traction-cell** plants of the CATL or BYD-blade type. Malaysia's battery cluster is weighted towards **cylindrical cells for tools and two-wheelers, and towards ESS**, which is the fastest-growing export category to the US and EU now that Chinese-origin ESS faces 301 tariffs and FEOC scrutiny.

**Mechanics of the shift**

Cell makers are moving the **"substantial transformation"** steps (electrode coating, cell assembly, formation) to Malaysia. They are also bringing their tier-1 materials suppliers along, which creates a "cluster-in-a-box" (抱团出海). Equipment (Lead Intelligent, Yinghe) and the first generation of engineers come from China.

**Watch-points**

- **US FEOC and "prohibited foreign entity" rules.** Under the 2025 US tax law, Chinese-owned plants in Malaysia may still be disqualified from US credits, wherever the plant is.
- **Rules-of-origin scrutiny.** The solar case (below) is the warning example.

### 3.2 Automotive and EVs (汽车)

**How the shift happened**

- **2017: Geely buys 49.9% of Proton** from DRB-HICOM. This was the anchor event. Geely platforms (Boyue → X70, Binyue → X50, and others) revived Proton. Its market share rose from about 13% to about 20% or more, and Proton became #2 in Malaysia.
- **2023–2025: Automotive High-Tech Valley (AHTV), Tanjung Malim.** This is a DRB-HICOM–Geely project of about **RM32bn** over roughly 10 years. Geely's target is **500,000 vehicles a year by 2035, half for export**, making Malaysia Geely's **RHD export hub**. (Geely reportedly shelved Thai EV plans in favour of Malaysia in 2023.)
  - In **September 2025**, Proton opened Malaysia's first dedicated EV plant at AHTV: 20,000 units a year in phase 1, rising to 45,000. It started with the **eMAS 7**, which had previously been imported CBU from China. **Zeekr** local assembly has been announced. Proton is moving its Shah Alam production to Tanjung Malim.
- **2023–2026: other Chinese OEMs enter.**
  - **BYD** (through Sime Darby) became the top BEV brand, first through CBU imports under the duty exemption. It then confirmed its own **CKD plant in Tanjung Malim**, on about 600,000 m², with **production due in 2H2026**, timed for the end of the CBU tax exemption.
  - **Chery** (Chery and Omoda/Jaecoo), **GWM**, **Changan**, **GAC Aion**, **Xpeng**, **Leapmotor** and **JAC** entered through CKD contract assembly, mostly at **Inokom (Kulim)**, **Tan Chong (Segambut)**, **Bermaz/Mazda Gurun**, or **HICOM Pegoh**.
- **Supplier pull.** Chinese tier-1s and tier-2s are following, for e-drive, wiring, batteries, glass and seats. Proton's Tanjung Malim engine plant can now build hybrid, PHEV and EV components, with a target of 400,000 units by 2028.

**Mechanics**

The pattern is a **step ladder: CBU import → CKD assembly → local parts → export platform.** Malaysia used **time-limited CBU duty exemptions as a forcing function**. Chinese OEMs entered with imports, built brand share, and then localised before the exemption ended.

**Watch-points**

- A brutal price war among Chinese brands is squeezing local dealers and Japanese incumbents.
- Local-content ratios remain low.
- Over-capacity risk: Malaysia's whole domestic market is about 800,000 vehicles a year.

### 3.3 Specialty and fine chemicals (精密化工)

**How the shift happened**

This is the **least advanced** of the four sectors, and the evidence is the thinnest.

- **Base layer: Petronas.** The **Pengerang Integrated Complex (PIC, Johor)** with Aramco, the **Kertih** and **Gebeng/Kuantan** clusters, and the ECER specialty-chemicals push give Malaysia feedstock that Vietnam and Indonesia lack. The anchor investors are Western and Korean (BASF-Petronas, Arkema, CJ Bio), not Chinese.
- **Chinese entries:**
  - **Rongsheng Petrochemical (荣盛石化):** a pledge of up to **RM80bn** (April 2023) for refining and chemicals in Pengerang. It is very large, but I could not confirm its current status, so treat it as a pledge, not capacity.
  - **Rianlon (利安隆)**, a polymer-additives maker, is building a plant and R&D centre at **Tanjung Langsat, Johor** (September 2025, more than 300 skilled jobs).
  - **Battery chemicals** (electrolyte, cathode, anode, binders) are the fastest-moving Chinese chemical investments. They overlap with §3.1: Capchem, Putailai, Hunan Yuneng.
  - **MCKIP / MCKIP-3 (Kuantan):** started with steel (Alliance Steel, 3.5 Mt) and is now pitched for downstream chemicals.
- **Electronic and process chemicals** (wet chemicals, photoresist ancillaries, specialty gases) are following the semiconductor cluster in Penang and Kulim, mostly at small scale.

**Why slower**

Chemicals need environmental approvals, hazardous-goods logistics, and site integration with feedstock. China's chemical clusters (Ningbo, Huizhou, Lianyungang) have enormous scale advantages. The tariff gap is also smaller than for batteries, solar and EVs, so the incentive to move is weaker.

### 3.4 Data centres (数据中心)

**How the shift happened**

- **Pre-2020:** Singapore was ASEAN's DC hub. In **2019 Singapore paused new DC approvals** because of land, power and carbon limits. Demand spilled across the causeway into **Johor**.
- **2021–2023: Chinese operators move early.** **GDS** (万国数据, through its international arm, later DayOne) built large campuses at **Nusajaya Tech Park and Kempas, Johor**. Investment there was reported at about RM14bn, plus a further RM1bn announced later. **Shanghai DC-Science** partnered with MN Holdings on a 120 MW Johor campus. **ByteDance**-linked capacity (leased and self-built), **Alibaba Cloud**, **Tencent Cloud** and **Huawei Cloud** regions followed. Malaysia became home to most Chinese-owned DC capacity outside mainland China.
- **2023–2025: the AI boom.** US hyperscalers (Microsoft, Google, AWS, Oracle) and NVIDIA-linked projects (YTL) joined. In the 12 months to Q2 2025, Johor's total pipeline nearly doubled to **5.8 GW**. Leasing was **260 MW in 1H2025, 61% of it from social-media platforms** (widely understood to include ByteDance/TikTok).
- **2025–2026: friction arrives.**
  1. **Chip export controls.** Reports said Chinese firms were training AI models on high-end NVIDIA GPUs in Malaysian DCs. From **14 July 2025**, Malaysia required **permits for export, transit and transhipment of US-origin AI chips** under the Strategic Trade Act, with 30 days' notice. Domestic use is not restricted, but the "rent compute offshore" model is now under scrutiny.
  2. **Power and water.** TNB's tariff reform raised base tariffs about 14% from July 2025, with special high-load-factor tariffs for DCs. In **November 2025**, during drought, Johor asked investors to **delay water-cooled expansions by about 18 months**. Approvals now require recycled water and sustainability measures.
  3. **US–Malaysia Agreement on Reciprocal Trade (October 2025).** Malaysia committed to align with US economic-security measures, including export controls. This narrows the room for the neutral position.

**Mechanics**

Unlike the factory sectors, DC migration was driven by **customer location and compute access**, not tariffs. Malaysia offered land and power close to Singapore's subsea-cable hub, light regulation, and, until 2025, a grey zone on GPU access.

## 4. Cross-cutting enabler: the solar precedent and trade defence

Solar is the clearest test of what happens when a shift is only "assembly arbitrage":

- After the 2012 US and EU duties on China, Chinese makers moved cell and module production to Malaysia, Vietnam, Thailand and Cambodia.
- In **2022–2023** the US found circumvention, and a two-year moratorium on new duties followed.
- In **2024–2025** new AD/CVD cases covered all four countries. Final rates (April 2025) reached **up to 168.8% for Malaysia** (with Jinko-Malaysia at about 41.6% combined) and as high as **3,404% for Cambodia**.
- Several Chinese-owned Southeast Asian lines were idled or moved again, to Indonesia, Laos, the Middle East and the US.

**The pattern:** a shift that adds little local value and depends on Chinese inputs gets **followed by the trade remedy within about 3–5 years.**

The same logic now drives US tariffs. Malaysia's reciprocal rate is **19%** (from August 2025), but **transhipped goods face up to 40%**. MITI has tightened certificate-of-origin checks.

## 6. Key lessons learned (经验与教训)

### For companies relocating (the Chinese firm's view)

1. **"Plus one" means rebuilding the whole cluster, not one factory.** The battery players that are succeeding (EVE plus its materials suppliers) moved **with their tier-1 ecosystem**. Firms that moved alone still imported 70–90% of their bill of materials from China and stayed exposed to origin challenges.
2. **Origin is a compliance capability, not a label.** Solar showed that "made in Malaysia" with Chinese wafers can lose its status overnight. Build traceability across inputs, the processing steps you perform locally (substantial transformation), and local value-content records **before** customs auditors ask. The US 40% transhipment rate makes this a board-level issue.
3. **Ownership now matters as much as location.** FEOC, "foreign entity of concern" and export-control rules look at **who owns and controls** the plant, not just where it is. Plans built only on location arbitrage need a second look. Some firms are using joint ventures, licensing, or minority stakes with Malaysian partners (Geely–DRB is a working model).
4. **Use local champions.** Geely–Proton is the clearest success. A respected national brand, government goodwill and an existing dealer network turned Chinese technology into a "Malaysian" product. That is politically safer and commercially faster than a stand-alone entry.
5. **Infrastructure limits come quickly.** Johor's DC boom ran into **power tariffs, grid capacity and water** within about four years. Lock in utilities (PPAs, recycled water, closed-loop cooling) as part of the site decision, not after it.
6. **Time-limited incentives are a deadline.** The EV CBU exemption worked: brands localised before it expired. Plan the CBU → CKD → local-content ladder against the incentive calendar.

### For Malaysia (the host-country view)

7. **Neutrality is an asset that needs maintenance.** Malaysia's appeal to Chinese firms depends on its access to Western markets. If it is seen as a conduit, through solar transhipment or GPU diversion, it is punished (tariffs, AD/CVD, chip-access limits), and that hurts **all** investors. The 2025 chip-permit rule and stricter certificate-of-origin checks were the right defensive moves.
8. **Measure value added, not approved ringgit.** Of RM17.9bn in Chinese manufacturing approvals (2023 to 1H2024), only about **49% of projects (RM3.8bn) had been realised**. Approvals are not capacity. Track realisation, local content, local engineering headcount and supplier linkages.
9. **Push for technology and talent transfer.** The best outcomes (Proton's engineering team, Penang's supplier base) came where local engineers and suppliers were built into the deal. Without that, Malaysia risks being the "assembly floor" for Chinese supply chains, the way it once was for US ones.
10. **Price scarce resources.** Data centres showed that underpriced power and water attract the most resource-hungry projects. The 2025 tariff reform and water rules came late, but they were needed.

### For multinationals and supply-chain planners (the buyer's view)

11. **Malaysia diversifies the location, not the supply chain's China dependency.** For risk planning, a Malaysian battery, EV or ESS supplier that is Chinese-owned and uses Chinese inputs is **geographically** diversified but **not politically** diversified. Map tier-2 and tier-3 inputs and ownership, not just the ship-from country.
12. **Plan for rules to change mid-contract.** Write tariff, origin and export-control change clauses into supply contracts, and qualify a second source outside the same ownership group for critical parts.

## 7. Outlook (2026–2030)

- **Batteries:** more ESS and materials capacity in Kedah and Johor. Whether EV traction cells follow depends on FEOC rules and on whether ASEAN EV demand reaches the scale to justify them.
- **Auto:** Malaysia's chance to be an RHD export hub depends on AHTV exports actually starting. The domestic market is too small for 10 or more Chinese brands, so expect consolidation.
- **Chemicals:** slow and selective, led by battery and electronic chemicals, with Rongsheng–Pengerang as the swing project.
- **Data centres:** growth continues, but shifts from "build anything" to power- and water-constrained, compliance-heavy AI capacity. Chinese operators face tighter GPU-access limits under the US–Malaysia trade agreement.
- **Biggest risk:** Malaysia gets caught in a US–China escalation that forces a choice. The second-biggest risk is that infrastructure (grid, water, talent) cannot keep up with approvals.
