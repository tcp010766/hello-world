# Teaching Note: China → Malaysia Supply-Chain Shift and the Geely × Proton Case

**For:** Operations Management (undergraduate, MBA or executive)
**Companion materials:** `china-to-malaysia-supply-chain-shift.md`, `geely-proton-case-study.md`
**Suggested length:** 2–3 sessions of 90 minutes

> **Before classroom use:** check the figures marked † in the case study against primary sources (MIDA, MAA, company filings). Cite the original sources, not these notes. Exercise numbers marked *(illustrative)* are made up for teaching and are not real company data.

---

## 1. Learning objectives

By the end, students should be able to:
1. Use **facility-location and global-network-design** frameworks on a real relocation decision.
2. Explain the difference between **geographic diversification** and **true supply-chain diversification** (who owns it, where the inputs come from, which technology it depends on).
3. Analyse **technology transfer, the learning curve and supplier development** as operational levers in a turnaround.
4. Build **total landed cost** and **break-even** models that include tariffs, rules of origin and incentive deadlines.
5. Assess **supply-chain risk** in a geopolitical setting: trade remedies, export controls, infrastructure limits.

## 3. Suggested module plan

### Session 1: The macro shift. Why Malaysia, and why now?
- **Pre-read:** report §1–§2 and §5 (timeline).
- **Opening question:** "If China is cheaper and more capable, why are Chinese firms building factories in Malaysia?"
- **Framework:** factor-rating model (Exercise A). Tie it to the three waves: cost → tariff → strategic.
- **Discussion:** do the four sectors (batteries, EVs, chemicals, data centres) move for the **same** reasons? (No. Batteries and EVs move because of tariffs and policy, data centres because of customer location and compute access, and chemicals barely move because they depend on feedstock and scale.)

### Session 2: The trade-remedy trap. Solar and batteries
- **Pre-read:** report §3.1 and §4.
- **Framework:** total landed cost with tariff scenarios (Exercise B). Use a **substantial-transformation** worksheet: which process steps happen where?
- **Discussion:** "Assembly-only relocations get hit by trade cases within 3–5 years." Is EVE Energy's Kulim plant more or less exposed than the Chinese solar plants were? Why does moving the supplier cluster matter?

### Session 3: Geely × Proton. Operations turnaround through capability transplant
- **Pre-read:** the full case study.
- **Cold call:** "In 2017 you are Proton's COO. Geely offers platforms, not cash. Do you accept?"
- **Board exercise:** list the operational levers Geely pulled (product, manufacturing, purchasing, distribution) and rank them by impact.
- **Exercises C and D:** local-content calculation, AHTV capacity break-even.
- **Debate:** "Proton in 2026 is a Malaysian badge on a Chinese car." Split the class for and against.
- **Wrap-up:** the lessons in §4.

## 4. Key lessons to teach

1. **Relocation ≠ diversification.** A plant moves location. Ownership, inputs, equipment and engineers often stay tied to China. Teach students to map **four layers**: location, ownership, bill-of-materials origin and technology origin.
2. **Total landed cost includes policy risk.** Tariffs, AD/CVD duties, rules-of-origin audits and incentive deadlines belong inside the cost model, not in an appendix.
3. **Origin is earned through process, not paperwork.** Substantial transformation, traceability and local value-add records are operational capabilities.
4. **Location advantages decay.** Johor's cheap power and water were used up within 4 years. Location decisions need scenarios for resource constraints.
5. **Cluster migration beats single-firm migration.** Co-locating suppliers lowers logistics cost, origin risk and ramp-up time.
6. **Turnarounds are mostly about operations.** Proton's recovery came from platforms, quality systems, purchasing scale and supplier development, not from a large capital injection.
7. **Phased localisation manages the learning curve.** CBU → CKD → local parts lets the market and the supply base develop together.
8. **Capability transfer has a cost: dependency.** Licensing technology is fast and cheap but can hollow out in-house R&D. Make-vs-buy decisions have long-run strategic consequences.
9. **Capacity must match the reachable market.** A 500k plan in an 815k home market is an export bet. Students should test the assumptions.
10. **Governance design is an operational tool.** The 49.9/50.1 split decided who ran operations and who handled politics.

## 5. Discussion questions

1. Rank the pull factors in report §2 for (a) a battery cell maker, (b) an EV OEM, (c) a hyperscale data-centre operator. Why do the rankings differ?
2. Would Geely's Proton playbook work for BYD, which is building a wholly owned plant? What does BYD gain and lose by going alone?
3. Proton keeps the Saga, its own in-house model. Why not replace the whole range with Geely platforms? Relate this to product-portfolio and capacity utilisation.
4. Which of Geely's levers (product, manufacturing, purchasing, distribution) would be hardest for a competitor to copy, and why?
5. You run procurement at a European OEM considering a Malaysian, Chinese-owned battery supplier. What due diligence do you do beyond the usual supplier audit?
6. Should Malaysia have required R&D localisation commitments in the 2017 deal? Design the clause.
7. Johor asked data centres to pause water-cooled expansions. How should a DC operator change its site-selection model?
8. Is "China+1" a stable equilibrium, or a temporary arbitrage that trade policy will eventually close?

## 6. Quantitative exercises

### Exercise A: Factor-rating location model *(illustrative weights and scores)*

Score each country from 1 to 10 for a new battery-cell plant serving US and EU customers:

| Factor | Weight | Malaysia | Vietnam | Thailand | Indonesia |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Labour cost | 0.10 | 6 | 8 | 6 | 8 |
| Skilled technicians | 0.15 | 8 | 6 | 7 | 5 |
| Power cost and reliability | 0.15 | 7 | 5 | 7 | 6 |
| Tariff and origin exposure to the US | 0.20 | ? | ? | ? | ? |
| Supplier ecosystem | 0.15 | 7 | 6 | 7 | 6 |
| Political neutrality | 0.10 | 8 | 6 | 7 | 7 |
| Raw materials (nickel) | 0.15 | 3 | 3 | 3 | 10 |

**Tasks:**
- Students fill in the tariff row from current US rates and compute the weighted scores.
- Run a **sensitivity analysis**: how much must the tariff weight rise before the ranking changes?
- Discuss what the model leaves out (ownership rules such as FEOC, for example).

### Exercise B: Total landed cost under tariff scenarios *(illustrative)*

A battery module made in Malaysia: ex-works cost US$100 and freight US$6. Inputs are 70% Chinese-origin. Compute the landed cost in the US under:
1. The reciprocal tariff of 19%.
2. A transhipment finding of 40%.
3. An AD/CVD order of 100%.
4. Raising the share of local inputs to 50%, at +US$8 cost, to secure origin.

**Question:** at what probability of scenario 2 or 3 does the localisation investment in scenario 4 pay off?

### Exercise C: Local content and the learning curve *(illustrative)*

The first CKD batch at Tanjung Malim takes 40 labour-hours per vehicle. Assume an **85% learning curve**.
- Hours for unit 1,000 and unit 10,000?
- If the target is 15 hours per vehicle, when does the plant reach it?
- Link the answer to CBU-exemption deadlines: when should the switch from CBU to CKD happen?

### Exercise D: AHTV capacity break-even *(illustrative)*

The plant has fixed costs of RM1.2bn a year at 500k capacity and a contribution margin of RM6,000 per vehicle. The domestic Geely-group share is assumed at 25% of an 815k market.
- What is the break-even volume?
- What export volume is needed to break even, and how does that compare with 2025 exports of about 6,000?
- Sensitivity: what happens if the domestic share falls to 18% because of the price war?

## 7. Research agenda: open questions for student projects, theses or faculty research

### A. Empirical: trade and relocation
1. **Value-added relocation vs assembly relocation.** Using OECD TiVA, ADB MRIO or UN Comtrade/BACI, measure whether rising Malaysian exports to the US in batteries, solar and EV parts reflect real domestic value-add, or rising imports of Chinese intermediates. (Look for HS-level "re-routing" signatures.)
2. **Speed of trade remedies.** Estimate the time from a surge in relocated exports to a US AD/CVD or circumvention case. Is 3–5 years a regularity across products?
3. **Ownership vs location in trade policy.** How do FEOC and connected-vehicle rules change the value of offshore Chinese plants? Event studies on the share prices of Chinese firms with ASEAN plants.

### B. Operations and strategy
4. **Capability transplant vs greenfield entry.** Compare Geely–Proton (minority stake in a local brand) with BYD (wholly owned), Chery (contract assembly) and Great Wall (bought a GM Thailand plant). Which mode ramps faster, reaches higher quality and builds more local content?
5. **Supplier development spillovers.** Did Proton's local vendors improve quality and productivity after 2017, and do they now supply other OEMs? (Data: MAA, vendor surveys, Bursa filings of listed suppliers.)
6. **Technology dependency over time.** Track Proton's in-house R&D headcount and patents before and after 2017. Does licensing crowd out or build absorptive capacity?
7. **Cluster migration.** Do battery-materials suppliers follow cell makers to Kedah in a predictable sequence and with a predictable lag? Network analysis of announced investments (fDi Markets, MIDA releases).

### C. Risk and sustainability
8. **Infrastructure as a binding constraint.** Model Johor's data-centre pipeline against grid and water capacity. When does the constraint bind, and what pricing would allocate it efficiently?
9. **Neutrality as a strategic asset.** Can a "neutral" host country stay neutral? Case comparison of Malaysia, Vietnam, Mexico and Hungary.
10. **Resilience measurement.** Build a supply-chain diversification index that counts ownership and input origin, not just ship-from country, and apply it to a sample of OEM supplier lists.

### Useful data sources
- **Investment:** MIDA annual reports and press releases, fDi Markets, Orbis/BvD (ownership).
- **Trade:** UN Comtrade, CEPII BACI, OECD TiVA, US Census trade data, US CBP rulings and EAPA circumvention cases, US ITC and Commerce AD/CVD determinations.
- **Auto:** Malaysian Automotive Association (MAA) monthly data, DRB-HICOM annual reports, Geely Automobile HKEX filings.
- **Energy and data centres:** Suruhanjaya Tenaga and TNB tariff documents, consultancy DC market reports (JLL, Cushman & Wakefield, Knight Frank). Note their definitions of live vs pipeline capacity.
